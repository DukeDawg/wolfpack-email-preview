Words from JJ Bonham

PC versus Liberty

When I was in my last year at the Yale School of Drama, one night in the playwright's class there began a discussion of the term political correctness. We threw around the term for half an hour or more, then I said to the class, “I think political correctness will be the biggest joke of the 20th Century.”

Unfortunately, what began as a political movement within Communism and the National Socialist Party and meant that basically something could be true, but if it did not agree with the political climate of that day, then it was politically incorrect. The party line had to be followed, facts be damned. It wasn't a joke then and it isn't a joke now. PC blossomed somehow into the Woke World. Double bubble, toil and trouble; fire burn and cauldron bubble is a famous line from the fourth act of the Scottish play and Google says it's the most recognized quote in English literature.

Let us gather round the cauldron and see what's cooking, shall we?

The joke it seems is on us, us being those who write whether for theatre or film, or books for that matter. What the PC world has done is drop a neutron bomb into our writing consciousness and now, we, fading fast from the fallout have turned ourselves into our very own Frankenstein's monster. Now, we, without outside interference, are the editors even before the work is finished, while we are writing! We don't wait to see how it will be received, no, we go ahead and cut, cut, cut – till something besides what we intended now stares back at us, but it's acceptable, right? I mean people won't get offended, right? What a shame, what a darn shame.

There is an expression in Buddhism which basically states that the lotus blossom is so beautiful because it has its roots in the mire at the bottom of the pond.

Within the western world that saying adapts to his boots were in the horseshit, but his hat was in the stars.

One of the reasons I was attracted to the theatre was because you could write what you wanted as in writing without worrying about people becoming offended. That, basically, hasn't changed. There's another medium in which this is true, the Holy Bible. I could get into Soren Kierkegaard's matter of the offense. How you can't really know Jesus until you've been offended by him and made the leap of faith over that offense, but I won't.

In another sense we in this modern era have entered the world of Marshall McLuhan when he said that the message we have to deliver as an extension of ourselves is nothing more than the new scale which is introduced when, we pick the medium we wish to use. Writing books is not the theatre, and I'm glad for that, but at the same time I wish the publishing world was more immune to being scared by what is on the page, get over itself and its offense and take the leap of faith with their authors.

Would Ken Follett's Pillars of the Earth have been published if political correctness had been involved? I seriously doubt it. And what would that book be without the rapes, murders, clergy wrongdoing? Just to throw out a specific example. When half-way through the book knights play a game of boredom called Kill the Cat, where a cat trapped in a room is continually bombarded with stones and the winner is the one who kills the cat. Would that have been stricken?

All this is to say, Uzziah & Immanuel – Texas Ranger Scouts is a story about two friends who both love and hate each other. Familiarity breeds contempt, right? Except they live in a time when freedom reigned. Men said what they said, and if you didn't like it, walk away or slug them in the mouth. Those were genuine choices.

I worked in a bar here in Hamilton, Montana that my daughter, Odessa used to own. Worked there for eight years. I ran into the most interesting people; I probably liked them because they were all drunks. Well, we are the most adorable of the addictions. And yes, I will have been in recovery for 47 years this 23rd day of December 2026. But I digress.

Peter O'Toole & Richard Burton, both gloriously in the middle of their alcoholic addictions gave us Becket. A king, Peter O'Toole decides to use his friend, Richard Burton, as a political appointment as the Archbishop of Canterbury, thinking that he will then be able to use the church's money for his wars. Yet, Becket, who has either grown a conscience or always had one behind that alcoholic blur, defies the king. It does not end well for the man who would not be king.

All I'm saying is that great story-telling spans the gaps between what is acceptable and what will possibly truly awaken a person, as in enlightenment!

Uzziah and Immanuel are not woke, but very much awake to the fact that life is a rumble sometimes, and if you choose not to get involved, then, well, you won't win that rumble and you might get beat anyway. They are two wonderfully glorious friends, who from different backgrounds and being very different people seem to make things work. Hey, that sounds like a good marriage, doesn't it?

Join these two irascible men as they pull no punches, make no amends and just let what's on their minds out of their mouths. I think you'll wish you were back there with them and could do the same.

JJ Bonham
