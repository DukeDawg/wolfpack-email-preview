Double Down

by W.L. Ripley

A stolen attaché case puts Las Vegas security man Conner McBride in the sights of a vicious crime boss. Rather than help the boss kill the robbers, McBride recruits a skilled operative and an enterprising showgirl for a dangerous scam. With federal agents, town bosses, and a killer from his past closing in, his next gamble could be his last.

Words from W.L. Ripley

How I Gambled on a Vegas Caper Novel

I had just written a Cole Springer adventure and was looking for something different. I’ve long been intrigued by Las Vegas and its myriad contradictions. Five-star hotels, exotic entertainment, swimming pools flourishing in the desert, thugs, cruds and mobsters in their wake and yet, Vegas is about as Americana as you get. I visited Vegas as a teen and then again, a few times as an adult. I was struck by the way the town had changed in just a few decades. From its foundation by the five families of the Mafia, through the Rat Pack and Elvis years, to the present family-oriented themes, Vegas has transformed itself again and again yet continues to thrive while becoming more dangerous than the Mob days.

There’s the ultimate contradiction—back in its halcyon days, the Mafia kept the tourists safe, happy, and fleeced, so the money would keep rolling back to them.

Enter Conner McBride, ex-Military Police captain, who is CEO of a faltering security business, who is struggling to stay above water and to complicate his trouble he owes a gambling debt to Sin City crime boss, Red Cavanaugh, a man he doesn’t like. Because of the debt McBride is pressured into finding two small-time crooks who have inadvertently stumbled into stealing a valuable attaché case belonging to Cavanaugh. Cole Springer readers will recognize the menacing Cavanaugh from the Springer series. Conner doesn’t really want to help Cavanaugh kill the bumbling wannabes and is looking for a way to recover the case, avoid bloodshed, and deny satisfaction to Cavanaugh. How does he accomplish that?

McBride, being the clever type, decides to scam Cavanaugh. It is a high-stakes gamble that could cost him everything, including his life.

Enter Trey Easton, rodeo star and movie stuntman who has fallen on hard times. Trey is the nephew of Wyatt Storme sidekick, Chick Easton. McBride hires Trey as his bodyguard and right-hand man for his security business. McBride only has one rule for Trey, telling the young man, “Nobody touches me,” a statement that will play an important role later.

Enter Aubrey Reynolds, an enterprising showgirl with brains and ambition who demands to be a part of the treasure hunt. Her trump card—Aubrey has the attaché case.

The mystery contents of the attaché case (à la PULP FICTION) are valuable and everyone wants inside it, including McBride. The contents of the case promise extreme riches for Cavanaugh and the devastation of an entire Central American country. It also promises reward bucks for McBride and Aubrey.

There are other characters who want the goods. J.J. Parks, McBride’s ex-wife living with a casino owner under Cavanaugh’s thumb; Hector Silvera, AKA “The Jaguar,” who is a Sandinista Colonel in league with Cavanaugh (and plans to double-cross him before Cavanaugh betrays him). There is also Lt. Tara St. John, a Metro Cop with designs on Trey, and LaRouche, a sadistic killer who nourishes a long-term grudge against McBride. Lt. St. John just wants to put Cavanaugh behind bars and burnish her résumé for a shot at Metro Captain.

Every player has a role and a personal vendetta/agenda. “It had more twists than Lombard St in San Francisco,” says one reader review. There are double crosses, triple crosses, romantic angst, three immoral criminals with intelligence, power, and determination to wipe out anyone in their path, along with two new heroes in McBride and Trey Easton. Easton, like his uncle, possesses a delightfully devilish sense of humor.

DOUBLE DOWN was a fun write for me. It is my hope you will enjoy reading it as much as I enjoyed composing it. Trey and McBride may possibly end up in a new adventure down the line.

W.L. Ripley
